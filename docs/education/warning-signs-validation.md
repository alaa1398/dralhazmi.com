# Warning signs guide: web validation

Date: 2026-11-27. Purpose: check the warning-signs guide (version 0.2, with your W1 and W2 decisions) against current published sources. Source: cloud session, Tavily web search (approved for this session) and PubMed. Pages were read in full where the site allowed it; snippet-only sources are marked.

## Summary

- **W1, heart rate over 120 is an emergency:** supported. The strongest source is a 518-patient gastric bypass study in which 7 of 8 leaks had sustained heart rates above 120. One US hospital's sleeve discharge sheet uses the same number.
- **W2, 38.0 °C call today and 38.5 °C emergency:** within the range US bariatric programs use (38.0 to 38.6 °C). Every program found tells patients to **call the surgeon** at these temperatures; none sends fever alone to the emergency department. Your 38.5 °C emergency line is stricter than theirs. That is safe, and may mean more emergency visits.
- **Every other item on the guide** matches at least one published source. Three findings bear on open decisions or suggest small additions (below).

## Item by item

| Item on the guide | What the sources say | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Heart racing at rest, over 120 beats a minute: emergency | Bellorin 2011 (518 bypass patients): 7 of 8 leaks (87.5%) had "sustained tachycardia above 120 bpm"; "Sustained tachycardia with a heart rate exceeding 120 bpm appears to be an indicator of anastomotic leak." Bleeding showed a gradual or cyclical rise that "never exceeded 120" in 60% of bleeding cases [S1]. NYU Langone sleeve discharge: call for "a fast heart rate, usually greater than 120 beats per minute", with how to count it (15 seconds × 4) [S2]. AdventHealth: "a persistent heart rate at or above 110" (search snippet only; page did not load) [S3]. | **Supported.** Caveat: bleeding can raise the heart rate without passing 120, so the guide's other red items (vomiting blood, black stools, fainting) still matter. |
| Temperature 38.0 °C or higher: contact the team today | Cedars-Sinai: contact the doctor for fever of 100.4 °F (38 °C) [S4]. UMass Memorial: fever of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher [S5]. NYU Langone: higher than 38.2 °C on 2 or more readings in weeks 1 to 3 [S2]. | **Supported.** |
| Temperature 38.5 °C or higher, or shaking chills: emergency | UCSF: "contact your surgeon immediately" for 101.5 °F (38.6 °C) or higher [S6]. Baylor: call for fever 101 °F (38.3 °C) or higher [S7]. Northwell: call if 101.5 °F or higher [S8]. UMass lists shaking chills [S5]. None of these routes fever alone to the emergency department. | **Consistent but stricter** than the sources (they say call; the guide says emergency). Your call; it errs on the safe side. |
| Normal: mild pain, tiredness, early fullness | Baylor: discomfort and abdominal pain in the first few days are expected; patients fill quickly [S7]. | Supported (patient instructions, not a guideline). |
| Nausea or vomiting that keeps coming back; cannot keep fluids down for (W3) hours | UCSF: contact your surgeon immediately for "nausea or vomiting that lasts more than 12 hours"; contact the team if vomiting continues more than 24 hours [S6]. NYU: call if you cannot drink at least 40 oz (about 1.2 L) a day for 2 days in a row [S2]. | Supported. **Bears on W3: 12 hours has a published precedent (UCSF).** |
| Drying out: dark or little urine, dizzy | UCSF: "inability to urinate lasting eight hours" [S6]. | Supported. Optional addition: "No urine for 8 hours." |
| Wound red, swollen, warm or leaking | UCSF, UMass, NYU all list it [S5, S6, S2]. | Supported. |
| Severe or worsening stomach pain: emergency | Leak and internal-hernia sources name abdominal pain first [S4, S10, S11]. | Supported. |
| Short of breath; any chest pain: emergency | Northwell: shortness of breath, difficulty breathing or chest pain, go to the emergency room immediately [S8]. Baylor: severe chest pain or trouble breathing, call the emergency number [S7]. Cedars-Sinai lists both as leak signs [S4]. | Supported. |
| One leg swollen, painful or warm: emergency | Northwell: calf pain and swelling, go to the emergency room [S8]. UCSF: contact your surgeon immediately [S6]. About 80% of VTE after bariatric surgery occurs after discharge (review citing ASMBS 2022) [S9]. | Supported; sources split between emergency room and "call now". The guide's emergency choice is the stricter one. |
| Vomiting blood; black, tar-like stools: emergency | Baylor: call for "poop that looks black or bloody" [S7]. | Supported (Baylor says call; the guide says emergency, stricter). |
| Confusion, hard to wake, fainting: emergency | UMass and NYU list confusion [S5, S2]; Wernicke review below [S10]. | Supported. |
| Tell staff about your surgery | Northwell: have the treating physician contact your surgeon; warns against blind stomach tubes [S8]. | Supported. |
| Later: days of vomiting with confusion, unsteady walking or double vision (vitamin B1) | Review of 118 cases after bariatric surgery: vomiting 87.3%, unsteady walking 84.7%, confusion 76.3%, eye movement problems 73.7%; 79.1% began in the first 6 months [S10]. | Supported. Optional key number to replace the removed "3–4 months": **"8 in 10 cases start in the first 6 months"** [S10]. |
| Later: bypass, sudden cramping pain that comes and goes: emergency | Expert review: post-meal pain with nausea or vomiting is the most common internal-hernia symptom; pain can be intermittent and a CT can be normal [S11]. | Supported. |

## Suggested changes (not made; for your approval)

1. Add Bellorin 2011 [S1] as a source for the heart-rate item and key number.
2. Add a one-line "how to count your pulse: count for 15 seconds, times 4" (NYU [S2]), since many patients cannot judge "over 120".
3. Add the Wernicke key number "8 in 10 start in the first 6 months" [S10].
4. Decide W3 and W5 using the findings above.
